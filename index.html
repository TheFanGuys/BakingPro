How to measure odd amounts

Grams (bold) are the accurate number — a kitchen scale removes all guesswork. The lighter number next to it is the closest practical spoon or cup combo.

Standard spoons only come in ⅛, ¼, ½, and 1 tsp (and ½, 1 tbsp). An amount like ⅜ tsp isn't a single spoon — it's ¼ tsp plus ⅛ tsp, added together. This app writes it that way instead of showing a fraction you can't actually measure. Amounts marked ● are close approximations — weigh them if it matters.

No ⅛ tsp spoon? Fill a ¼ tsp about halfway, or use a rounded pinch between two fingers — about ⅛ tsp. For a true dash under that, it's a light pinch.

Where these numbers come from

Buttermilk biscuits, the Paula Deen-style variant, blueberry biscuits, corn tortillas, egg noodles, and basic egg pasta were cross-checked against several published recipes; where sources disagreed, I used a middle value. All three pizza doughs use standard baker's-percentage ranges checked against multiple sources: New York style around 62% water/2% salt/2% oil/0.5% yeast; Neapolitan around 60% water/3% salt/no oil/very little yeast, per the official AVPN ratios; Detroit style around 75% water/2% salt/2% oil, no-knead. Dough-ball and pan weights are typical, not exact. Cornbread, pancakes, flour tortillas, pie crust, and sausage gravy use standard ratios but haven't been cross-checked against multiple sources yet.

Baking powder/soda cuts at altitude follow Utah State University Extension. Extra liquid and oven bumps follow Betty Crocker and Cook's Country guidance for quick breads. These only apply to true quick breads (biscuits, cornbread, pancakes) — not yeast dough, pasta, tortillas, noodles, pie crust, or gravy, which don't use baking powder or aren't altitude-sensitive the same way. Pizza yeast is not numerically reduced here — the reliable altitude advice for yeast is to shorten the rise by watching the dough, not to cut the yeast by a fixed percent. Above 7,000 ft the quick-bread numbers are stretched past what the sources cover.

Flour is measured spooned and leveled (120 g/cup). No recipe here has been bake-tested by me. Bake a batch before you trust it for a job.

Bread & chef-pizza sources

Breads: the Whole Wheat Sandwich Bread is King Arthur's published "Classic 100% Whole Wheat Bread." The Whole Wheat Sourdough Sandwich Bread and the Whole Wheat Sourdough Pita are both real, published formulas from The Perfect Loaf (Maurizio Leo) — I checked the gram totals against the source and they add up. The 100% Whole Wheat Sourdough (hearth loaf) is my own adapted formula, built from the hydration and levain-ratio principles in The Perfect Loaf's sourdough guide — not a single named recipe of theirs. The Whole Wheat No-Knead Bread is adapted from Jim Lahey's original No-Knead Bread method (New York Times, 2006) using a whole-wheat version documented by home bakers, with vital wheat gluten added for structure. Every bread's whole-grain percentage is computed from its actual flours, including the flour used to feed the starter — not just the flour added at the dough stage.

Chef pizzas: Pizzeria Bianco's dough (Chris Bianco) and Roberta's dough are both real published recipes — Bianco's from Food & Wine, Roberta's originally from NYT Cooking. Lucali's dough (Mark Iacono) is from his own published recipe via Good Morning America/ABC News — I found that the ingredient amounts as published sum to noticeably more than the stated 8×10oz portioning, so I flag that instead of quietly forcing the math to fit. The Tony Gemignani-style dough is adapted: his linked article is home-oven shopping and technique guidance (flour choice, diastatic malt, the two-steel method), not a numbered formula, so the percentages here follow the same range he describes for high-protein home-oven dough rather than his exact published figures.

For any pizza flour, "unbleached" alone doesn't mean unenriched — check the ingredient label on the specific bag you buy for added niacin, iron, thiamine, riboflavin, or folic acid, which mean it's enriched.
